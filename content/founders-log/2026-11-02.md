## Substack post

**Title:** The ads were fine. I was the leak.

**Subtitle:** Founder's Log 04. I went looking for a broken ad and found 57 people we never finished talking to.

Last week I wrote that I cut our posting by two thirds and would know in four weeks if I was right. That clock is still running. This week I got distracted by a better question.

[Alex: adjust the scene. Where were you when you watched the funnel video, and what made you stop and pull up the numbers?]

I was watching a marketer walk through how he finds what is broken in a funnel. His rule is simple. Look at one number first. Only if it is bad, break it into steps. Then fix the one step that is furthest from where it should be. Not the one that is easiest to argue about.

So I did it with our ads, step by step.

How cheap it is to reach people: fine. How many of them click: fine. How many clicks turn into a lead: fine, on our best ad.

Then the last step. Of the people who filled out our form, how many actually booked?

About 8 percent. Five out of 62, over four weeks.

The benchmark for a business like ours is somewhere between 15 and 25 percent. We were at half of the bottom of the range.

And when I looked closer, 57 of those 62 people were still sitting in our system marked "open." Weeks later. Not booked, not lost, not given a follow up date. Just open.

---

I had spent weeks testing ad designs. Colors, headlines, a news style layout, which reason people pick us. Meanwhile the real leak was after the ad. It was us.

So we made a call. We stopped testing new ads for the rest of October. One proven ad, $40 a day, nothing else. And all the energy went to the follow up: making sure our texts actually land on people's phones, answering fast with a real person, and putting every open lead into a real stage.

The ad held steady. It spent about $170 over four days and brought in 7 leads, about $24 each. A little worse than its best weeks. I am watching it, not touching it.

---

Here is the mistake, and I am telling it because it is the whole point of this week.

I draft a lot of the replies we send to people asking about boarding. One afternoon a mom asked about a half day for her dog. My draft had a blank in it, a placeholder for the price, because I didn't know her dog's weight yet. The text went out with the blank still in it. Brackets and all.

She was gracious about it. We fixed it in the next message. She booked.

But I sat with it. I had just spent a week proving that our follow up is where we lose people, and here I was, in the follow up, sending a message that looked like a form letter.

The fix is boring. No blanks in a draft, ever. If a fact is missing, ask for it first, or write two finished versions.

That same week I almost lost another booking a different way. A returning client asked about daytime care. My first draft said we only do overnights. True on paper. But if we had sent it, he would have gone somewhere else. What we actually do is take day visits once in a while, at our normal rate. One sentence would have cost us a regular client.

Both mistakes were in the last ten feet. The part after the ad, after the click, after the form. The part I thought was handled.

---

The other thing that changed this week came from the opposite end.

I have been studying a few dog trainers whose videos get hundreds of thousands of views. The pattern is not cute dogs. It is teaching. Why a dog does what it does. What owners get wrong. One trainer who runs boarding and board and train, the same model we are building, got about 8 million views on a normal day of caring for his dogs, just narrating what he noticed about each one.

So our page is turning into a place where people learn about dogs. Training, breeds, puppies, older dogs, even cats and the exotic pets we board. The reason to follow us becomes: you will learn something every day. The reason to book us comes later, and it comes from trust.

I don't know yet if it will work for a local business. I know the old way was not working well enough.

---

What I am taking from this week:

I kept looking for the problem where it was interesting to look. Ads are interesting. Designs are interesting. The follow up is not interesting. That is exactly why it was broken.

What I don't know: whether 8 percent moves toward 15 now that we are paying attention to it. I will have the first real read in late November.

If you run a business, reply and tell me where your "last ten feet" is. The part you assume is fine because you set it up once. I read every one.

Alex
