## Substack post

**Title:** I asked which ad would win. Instagram never showed them.

**Subtitle:** Founder's Log 03. The pretty ads lost, the ugly one kept winning, and I cut my posting by two thirds on purpose.

Last week I ended this log with a promise. I said that by Saturday I would know which reason people choose us for. Our reviews, how we care for each dog, or the honest line about not being the cheapest.

Saturday came and I opened the numbers. [Alex: adjust the scene. Where were you when you looked, and what did you feel first?]

There was no winner.

Not because the three reasons tied. Because Meta barely showed any of them. Nine clean, cream colored ads, built to look like our brand. Each one spent under $17 in a month. Most brought in zero leads. One brought in one.

I had been waiting all week for an answer to a question nobody got to vote on.

---

Here is what was happening at the same time.

The ad that has carried us for six months is not pretty. It looks like a local news article. A masthead, a long headline, two photos, a wall of review cards. If you saw it in your feed you would think someone wrote about us.

Over the last 30 days it spent about $1,050 and brought in 57 leads. About $18 each.

And one more ad, an old one about drop off, brought in 8 leads at about $9 each. Its whole message is one sentence. Pull up, text us, and we bring your dog to your car.

So the data said something I did not want to hear. On Facebook and Instagram, an ad that looks like a real post beats an ad that looks like a brand. Proof that feels native wins. Polish gets skipped.

I like our brand colors. I still do. But I was designing for myself, not for a dog parent scrolling at 11 at night.

So we made the news style our standard. We rebuilt it with our own name on the masthead, never a real paper's name, because faking press would undo the one thing the ad is selling, which is trust. Then we started testing versions of it against each other.

---

The second thing I learned this week was worse, and it was about our own Instagram.

We were posting about 15 times a week. The accounts like ours that are growing post about 3.

Each of our posts got about 0.27% engagement. Theirs got 2.49%. Nine times more, on a fifth of the posts. We ranked in the 8th percentile.

I thought volume was the answer. Post more, get seen more. What actually happens is Instagram shows each new post to a small group first. When most of them scroll past, it learns your followers do not care, and shows the next post to fewer people. We were teaching it to ignore us, 15 times a week.

So on Monday we cut to one post a day, at noon. Plus two short Stories a day, one in the morning and one at night. Any extra videos get tested first with people who do not follow us yet, and only the winner goes on our page.

It feels wrong to post less in a year where my whole job is getting more people to know we exist. I am doing it anyway, and I will know in four weeks if I was right.

---

What I am taking from this week:

I asked the wrong question. I wanted to know which message wins, and I never checked if the message was being delivered at all. Before you judge what people chose, make sure they actually saw the choice.

And more is not always more. Sometimes more is just noise that trains people to look away.

Here is what I do not know yet. Whether the news style keeps winning once we have six versions of it running. And whether posting a third as much will grow us or quietly shrink us. I will tell you both, with numbers, as they come in.

If you run a small business, reply and tell me one thing you do every week out of habit that you have never checked is working. I read every one.

Alex
