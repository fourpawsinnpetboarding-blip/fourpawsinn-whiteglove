## Substack post (story version, Oct 11 rewrite)

**Title:** The bill I paid twice for seven months

**Subtitle:** Founder's Log 01. A Friday night, two identical accounts, and the week our ads finally learned who our customers are.

It was a little after ten on a Friday night when I found it.

The house had gone quiet the way it does once the last dogs settle down for the night. Amanda was done for the day. I should have been done too. I had a laptop open on the counter with too many tabs: our CRM, a Meta dashboard, a spreadsheet, a chat window with an AI. I was doing the most boring job in business. Updating a credit card.

I logged out and logged back in, and the screen asked me to choose an account.

Two names. Same name, same company. Four Paws Inn, and Four Paws Inn.

I always clicked the bottom one. I had never once asked what the top one was.

So I clicked it. Empty. No contacts, no conversations, no bookings. A dashboard full of zeros and a default sales pipeline someone had never touched. It looked like a house with the lights on and nobody home.

Then I opened my Amex app and searched the company name. Seven charges. Ninety seven dollars every month since April, plus ten dollars in March. Six hundred eighty nine dollars for a room nobody was living in.

I would love to tell you I stayed calm. I did not. I typed things into that support chat at ten o'clock that I am glad nobody screenshotted.

Here is what bothered me most. It was not the money. It was that I am the person in my business who is supposed to see this kind of thing. And I did not see it, because both accounts had the same name and the second card was one I never look at.

---

That was the end of the week. The start of the week was better.

For months our ads had been doing their job. People filled out a form, we texted them back, some of them booked. A few of them stayed with us for weeks.

But the ad platform never knew who booked. It only knew who filled out a form. So every day it went out and found me more people who like filling out forms. That is not the same as people who book.

This week that changed. Now when a family books and pays their deposit, our system tells Meta: this one became a customer. Before October 8, the number of bookings we had reported back was zero. By Friday it was fourteen.

It is a strange feeling. Nothing looked different. The phone did not ring more that afternoon. But somewhere, the machine that spends my ad money started learning the right lesson.

---

The part I keep thinking about is a conversation with Amanda.

We built a booking sheet over two years. It runs our whole money side: who is staying, what they owe, what we made that day. I genuinely love that sheet. It is the closest thing our business has to a heartbeat monitor.

To get bookings to talk to our ads, I asked her to type a few more things into it for every booking. Owner name. Email. Where they heard about us.

She pushed back. Hard. She said it was too much.

My first reaction was frustration. My second reaction, a few minutes later, was that she was right. She is on the phone with a client, sending a payment link, answering the door. Every extra field is one more thing between her and the next family.

So instead of asking her to work harder, we made the sheet do it. Now she marks the deposit as paid, the booking fills itself in from the client's intake form, and she types one number. The daily rate. That is it.

I made a copy of the whole sheet first. I was scared of breaking the thing I love. Nothing broke.

---

So here is where I am, a week in.

I am not writing this because I have it figured out. I am writing it because I don't, and I want a record of what it actually looks like to figure it out. The late nights, the wins nobody sees, and the mistakes that cost real money.

What I learned this week, for whatever it is worth:

Check every card you own once a month for every tool you pay for. Count the charges.

And the machines you hire, whether that is an ad platform or an AI, can only learn from what you show them. If you only show them the easy part, that is all they will ever get good at.

Next week I am opening a waitlist for something new, a training program for the dogs who stay with us. I have no trainer yet. I don't know if anyone will sign up. I will tell you either way.

If you run a pet business, or any small business, reply and tell me what kept you up this week. I read every one.

Alex

## YouTube script (teleprompter, about 4 minutes, film Sunday)

Title options:
1. My ads ran for months and Facebook never knew who booked
2. I paid for the same software twice for 7 months
3. Founder's Log 01: running my dog boarding business with AI

**[Cold open, look at camera]**
For months my ads were spending money every single day.
And Facebook had no idea which of those leads actually paid me.
This week I fixed that.
And I found a bill I had been paying twice.

**[Intro]**
I'm Alex. My wife Amanda and I run Four Paws Inn, a home based dog boarding business in Miramar, Florida.
I'm not a developer. I run this business with two AIs, Claude and Codex.
This is Founder's Log number one. Every week: the numbers, one win, one mistake, and the rule I made from it.

**[Numbers]**
Here are this week's numbers.
57 leads came in from our Facebook and Instagram forms in the last 30 days.
Bookings we reported back to Meta before October 8: zero.
Since October 8: fourteen.

**[Win]**
So here's the win.
When a lead fills out a form, Meta counts that as a success.
But a form is not a customer.
If you only tell Meta who filled out a form, it goes and finds you more people who fill out forms.
So now, when someone books and pays a deposit, our CRM marks it Won, and sends that straight to Meta.
Now Meta learns what a paying customer looks like.
And we took the typing off my wife's plate.
Our booking sheet used to take about ten fields per booking.
Now she marks the deposit paid, the row fills itself in, and she types one number.
The rest runs on its own.

**[Mistake]**
Now the mistake.
I was updating a card on our CRM, and I found two accounts.
Same name. Same login. Same company.
One was the account we use every day.
The other one was completely empty. Zero activity.
And it had been charging a different card. For seven months.
Six hundred eighty nine dollars. For nothing.
I never noticed, because the account looked identical, and that card is one I never check.

**[Lesson]**
So here's the rule.
Once a month, search every card you own for every software name.
Count the charges.
If you count more charges than tools you actually use, you're paying twice.
And the bigger lesson: your ad platform can only learn from what you tell it.
Tell it who pays you. Not just who clicks.

**[Cliffhanger]**
Next week, I'm opening a waitlist for a new training program.
No trainer hired yet.
I'll show you how many people join, and what I do with that number.

**[Close]**
If you run a pet business, comment and tell me your biggest headache.
I read every one.
See you next week.
